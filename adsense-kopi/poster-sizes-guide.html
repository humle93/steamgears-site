Poster Sizes Explained: 30×40, 50×70 or 70×100 cm?

Updated 2026-11-14

The short answer: 30×40 cm for shelves, hallways and gallery walls, 50×70 cm for a single piece above a desk, bed or sideboard, and 70×100 cm when one print has to hold a whole wall. The rest of this guide explains how to check that against your own room in two minutes, with a tape measure and a roll of masking tape.

The three sizes that cover almost every wall

Most frames sold in Europe are built around a handful of standard poster sizes, and three of them do nearly all the work:

  • 30×40 cm — the workhorse. Small enough for a narrow hallway, a bookshelf or a bathroom, and the easiest size to combine in pairs or threes.
  • 50×70 cm — the most popular single-print size. It reads clearly from across a room without dominating it, and it is the natural choice above a desk, a bed or a chest of drawers.
  • 70×100 cm — the statement size. Use it where you would otherwise hang a mirror or a canvas: above a sofa, at the end of a corridor, or as the one big piece in a study.

All three share the same 3:4 to 5:7 portrait shape, so a design looks the same in every size — only the distance it works from changes.

How big should a poster be above a sofa or bed?

Interior designers use a simple rule: art above furniture should be about two-thirds of the furniture’s width. A 200 cm sofa wants roughly 130 cm of art across. One 70×100 cm print is a little narrow on its own, so the usual answers are:

  • one 70×100 cm print for sofas and beds up to about 160 cm wide,
  • two 50×70 cm prints side by side (with 5–8 cm between them) for 180–220 cm sofas,
  • three 50×70 cm prints in a row for a long sideboard or a wide double bed.

Leave 20–30 cm between the top of the furniture and the bottom of the frame. Any more and the print starts to float away from the piece it belongs to.

Viewing distance: the quick check

A print should be readable from where you normally see it. A rough guide is that the long side of the print should be about one-third to one-half of your usual viewing distance:

  • seen from 1 m (a hallway, beside a desk): 30×40 cm is plenty,
  • seen from 2 m (across a bedroom, from a desk chair): 50×70 cm,
  • seen from 3 m or more (across a living room): 70×100 cm, or a group of smaller prints.

The masking-tape test

Before you order, cut a piece of paper or tape out the outline of the size you are considering on the wall itself. Leave it up for a day. It sounds fussy, but it is the single most reliable way to avoid a print that looks like a postage stamp on a big wall — or one that crowds a small room.

How high to hang a poster

Galleries hang art so that the centre of the picture sits about 145–150 cm from the floor, roughly eye height for most adults. Above furniture, ignore the floor and measure from the furniture instead (the 20–30 cm gap above). For a group of prints, treat the whole group as one picture and put its centre at 145–150 cm.

Frames and passe-partout

A standard-size print fits an off-the-shelf frame of the same size, which keeps framing cheap and fast. If you want a mount (passe-partout), buy the next frame size up: a 30×40 cm print in a 40×50 cm frame with a mount, or a 50×70 cm print in a 70×100 cm frame. A mount gives a smaller print the presence of a larger one and works especially well with detailed, engraved or botanical artwork.

For steampunk and vintage designs, warm frames suit the palette: dark wood, black, brushed brass or copper all pick up the metal tones in the artwork without competing with it.

Mixing sizes on one wall

Two rules keep a mixed wall calm:

  1. Keep one thing constant. Same frame colour, same mount, or the same artwork series — if everything varies at once, the wall looks accidental.
  2. Keep the gaps equal. 5–8 cm between frames works for most walls. Equal spacing matters more than equal sizes.
