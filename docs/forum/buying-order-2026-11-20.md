## Brødtekst (HTML)

```html
<p>Most first costumes are bought in the wrong order, and the order is why they end up costing more than they
should. The pattern is always the same: the goggles arrive first because they are the fun part, then the coat
arrives and the brass on it is a different brass, and then a second pair of goggles is bought to match. Two
pairs of goggles, one coat, and the outfit still does not read.</p>
<p>Buy in the order below and every purchase constrains the next one instead of contradicting it.</p>

<h2>1. Footwear and trousers</h2>
<p>Start at the floor, because shoes decide the era faster than any prop. Leather boots push you towards
captain, explorer or maritime; dress shoes push you towards scholar or gothic. Most people already own
something that works, which is why this step is usually free — and why it should be settled before anything is
bought. A costume with the wrong shoes cannot be rescued by a better hat.</p>

<h2>2. The outer layer</h2>
<p>A coat, a waistcoat or a corset carries more of the silhouette than everything else combined, and it sets the
palette you will be matching for the rest of the build. Buy it second and you buy it once. Buy it fourth and you
will be choosing between returning it and replacing the accessories that no longer match. This is the piece
worth spending on.</p>

<h2>3. Goggles or headwear</h2>
<p>This is the genre switch: nothing converts ordinary clothes into steampunk faster. But it only works if the
metal agrees with the outer layer. Once you know whether your coat's hardware is warm brass or cooler
nickel, the goggles choose themselves — and that is the whole reason they come third rather than first.</p>

<h2>4. One metal accent</h2>
<p>A pocket watch, a compass, a chain. One. Not two, and definitely not a belt covered in gears. A single brass
object reads as intent; several read as a costume shop, and the eye stops being able to tell which one
matters.</p>

<h2>5. Gloves, scarf, small props</h2>
<p>Last, because they are the easiest to substitute from what you own and the easiest to make. A plain leather
glove and a knitted scarf do more for a cold-weather look than any prop, and neither needs to match anything
exactly.</p>

<h2>The budget split that follows from this</h2>
<p>If the whole build has a fixed budget, roughly: <strong>40 % outer layer, 25 % goggles, 20 % base layers and
trousers, 15 % accent.</strong> It looks lopsided until you stand in front of a mirror, at which point the coat
and the goggles are the only two things a stranger can identify from across a room.</p>

<h2>What not to buy first</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Gear clusters and rivet sheets.</strong> They are the last 5 % of a build and the first thing
beginners buy.</li>
<li><strong>Anything sold as a "steampunk kit".</strong> Kits are priced for convenience and matched by
nobody.</li>
<li><strong>Toy rayguns and oversized props.</strong> Convention prop rules will decide whether they are allowed,
and you cannot know that until you have picked the venue.</li>
<li><strong>A wig.</strong> It changes the character, not the genre, and it is the most common regret
purchase.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The rule that stops the spending</h2>
<p>When the third brass item arrives, stop. Three pieces is a costume; five is a collection you carry in a bag
and never wear comfortably for eight hours. If you want one more thing after that, it should replace something,
not join it.</p>

<p>The full order, with what each step costs at three budget levels, is in
<a href="https://steamgears.eu/steampunk-cosplay-on-a-budget/">steampunk cosplay on a budget</a>. If you are
still choosing a character rather than a shopping list, the
<a href="https://steamgears.eu/start-here/">start page</a> routes you to the guides by occasion and archetype,
and <a href="https://steamgears.eu/steampunk-accessories/">the accessories guide</a> covers the pieces that
finish a look, including the ones worth making yourself.</p>
<p>Curious how other people did it: what did you buy first, and what did you end up replacing?</p>
```
